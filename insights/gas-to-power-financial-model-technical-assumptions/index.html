Gas-to-Power Financial Model: Technical Assumptions That Can Break the Economics

A technical review of capacity, fuel, availability, maintenance and lifecycle assumptions that can make a gas-to-power financial model misleading.

Igor Shibanov · Founder and Technical AdvisorPublished: 4 October 2026Last reviewed: 9 October 2026
MODEL REVIEWENGINEERING INPUTSSENSITIVITY

Key takeaways

  • A gas-to-power model is only as credible as its fuel, net-output, utilization, maintenance and replacement assumptions.
  • Gross nameplate output, net export and sellable energy are different quantities and should never share one unlabeled model input.
  • Availability, utilization and reliability describe different things; each metric needs a definition, time basis and source.
  • The model should expose uncertainty through sensitivities instead of hiding unsupported engineering assumptions behind a single base case.

Which technical assumptions most often distort a gas-to-power model?

Fuel composition and pressure, ambient derating, parasitic loads, planned maintenance, forced downtime, overhaul timing and replacement scope are frequent sources of distortion. The review should trace each material input to a document, measurement, calculation or clearly labeled benchmark and test whether all inputs use the same plant boundary.

What makes a financial-model review decision-grade?

A decision-grade review identifies the small number of engineering inputs that control value, checks their evidence and units, and shows how the economics move when those inputs change. It does not provide investment advice; it establishes whether the technical basis is internally consistent and suitable for the stated decision.

A gas-to-power model is only as credible as its technical inputs. Small errors in net output, fuel energy, utilisation or maintenance timing compound across the project life and can create returns that the physical plant cannot deliver.

This article addresses the engineering inputs. It does not provide financial or investment advice.

The commercial and technical problem

The spreadsheet usually begins with installed megawatts, gas consumption and an availability percentage. Those values often arrive from different sources and use different boundaries: gross engine output, ideal reference gas, contractual availability, observed utilisation or a maintenance schedule for another configuration.

If the definitions are not reconciled, the model can be mathematically correct and operationally wrong.

Why the issue matters

Fuel cost and saleable electricity dominate many projects, but both depend on measurement boundaries. Maintenance is not a smooth annual percentage: it arrives in scheduled events, major interventions and uncertain corrective work. External interruptions can reduce production even when the engine is healthy.

A technical review should show a base case, a downside case and the evidence class behind each key input.

What should actually be checked

  • Define installed, gross, auxiliary, net and dependable capacity separately.
  • Model the actual dispatch profile rather than assuming continuous rated output.
  • Use a fuel-consumption basis tied to a stated gas lower heating value, reference conditions and engine configuration.
  • Separate measured gas data from normalized performance-curve benchmarks.
  • Define utilisation, technical availability, contractual availability and reliability before using a percentage.
  • Schedule maintenance events and overhaul cash requirements at the applicable operating hours rather than smoothing them without explanation.
  • Include planned downtime, start/stop behaviour, ambient derating and site auxiliary loads.
  • Model fuel-supply, grid, consumer and weather constraints where the project boundary carries those risks.
  • Separate scheduled maintenance reserve from total O&M and from commercial service tariffs.
  • Stress unverified inputs instead of treating them as facts.

Common mistakes and red flags

  • Dividing electricity by an unverified gas-meter total and calling the result engine efficiency.
  • Applying a brochure heat rate without matching gas composition or reference conditions.
  • Using nameplate capacity as annual net output.
  • Treating a single availability value as an equipment property.
  • Ignoring the maintenance cash-flow cliff at major service events.
  • Mixing historical price lists, current quotes and assumptions without labels.

Relevant evidence and its limits

In K4-Technology’s published three-J320 operating case, gas consumption was recorded by site meters, but the implied consumption was materially below the passport reference. K4-Technology did not use the meter-derived figure to claim actual efficiency. The investment calculation used passport consumption instead, while the exact meter value remained unpublished.

The same case classified 806 lost unit-hours across fuel supply, ambient heat, weather, maintenance, repairs, grid or consumer events and unclassified records. This is why a model should not reduce every outage to engine availability.

K4-Technology’s published J320 lifecycle page demonstrates the discipline of separating historical OEM baseline, time-specific current gross pricing, recorded procurement and K4-Technology assumptions. Its figures apply to the stated J320 D25 / 320_C natural-gas basis; they are not universal gas-engine costs.

Practical decision checklist

  • Does every input have a unit, boundary, source and date or applicability statement?
  • Can measured values be reconciled across the same time period and equipment boundary?
  • Are maintenance events visible in both cash flow and production loss?
  • Does the downside case reflect external constraints as well as equipment events?
  • Are unresolved inputs converted into sensitivities and verification actions?

When remote review is sufficient

A remote Financial Model Review can examine the technical input sheet, performance basis, maintenance schedule, fuel assumptions and sensitivity logic without visiting the site, provided the source documents and model are available.

When physical inspection is required

Physical verification is required when the model depends on actual meter accuracy, installed auxiliaries, site derating, fuel sampling, equipment condition or operating practices that records cannot establish.

Conclusion

The goal is not a more complicated model. It is a model whose important numbers describe the same plant, under the same conditions, across the same boundary.
