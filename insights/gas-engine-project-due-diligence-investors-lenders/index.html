Gas Engine Project Due Diligence for Investors and Lenders

A practical technical due-diligence framework for investors and lenders evaluating gas-to-power projects, from fuel and equipment to O&M and acceptance.

Igor Shibanov · Founder and Technical AdvisorPublished: 4 October 2026Last reviewed: 3 October 2026
OWNER-SIDE REVIEWK4 ANALYSISNDA-SAFE SUMMARY

Key takeaways

  • Technical due diligence should connect the financial model to the physical plant, fuel source, maintenance plan and acceptance evidence.
  • A plausible equipment datasheet does not prove that a project can deliver the modeled net output or operating profile.
  • Material gaps should become priced conditions, verification actions or investment hold points rather than generic risk language.
  • Remote review can reject an incoherent project basis early; site work is reserved for conclusions that depend on installed condition or witnessed tests.

What should gas-engine project due diligence test first?

Start with the chain from fuel quantity and quality to net electrical output, operating hours, maintenance exposure and cash flow. Confirm that all assumptions describe the same site, equipment configuration, ambient conditions and system boundary. If the source documents cannot support that chain, the model is not yet decision-grade.

When is an owner-side technical review most valuable?

It is most valuable before capital, equipment deposits or binding project milestones are committed. At that point, missing evidence can still be requested, specialist checks can be targeted and commercial conditions can be written around unresolved fuel, equipment, grid, construction, commissioning or operating risks.

A gas-to-power model can be financially coherent and technically fragile at the same time. The risk usually sits at the interfaces: the fuel specification does not match the engine basis, gross output is treated as saleable output, maintenance downtime is missing, or responsibilities between supplier, EPC contractor, operator and offtaker do not close.

Technical due diligence should therefore test the complete operating system and the evidence behind each material assumption. It is not an OEM certificate and it is not financial or investment advice.

The commercial and technical problem

Capital is committed against a chain of assumptions: fuel quantity and quality, equipment configuration, site conditions, net capacity, operating profile, maintenance plan, spare-parts access, grid or captive-load interface and the competence of the delivery parties.

A project can fail even when the selected engine is technically capable. Fuel interruptions, ambient derating, grid events, incomplete commissioning and weak operating records can reduce realised output without appearing as an engine failure.

Why the issue matters

For an investor, an unsupported technical assumption changes expected cash flow, reserve requirements and the conditions that should precede drawdown. For a lender, it changes completion tests, covenant definitions and the evidence needed to distinguish project underperformance from an equipment defect.

The purpose of technical due diligence is not to predict every failure. It is to identify which assumptions are verified, which are contractual, which are analytical and which remain unsupported.

What should actually be checked

  • Define the project boundary: fuel receipt, gas treatment, generation, heat rejection, electrical export or captive load and all auxiliary systems.
  • Reconcile the proposed engine model and configuration with the actual fuel composition, pressure, variability and contaminant-control plan.
  • Separate gross installed capacity from dependable net output after auxiliaries, ambient conditions, degradation and reserve philosophy.
  • Test the operating profile: base load, load following, island operation, grid parallel, start frequency and minimum-load exposure.
  • Review maintenance intervals, major-overhaul assumptions, planned downtime, labour capability and spare-parts logistics.
  • Verify that EPC scope closes every interface between engine package, gas system, cooling, ventilation, electrical balance of plant, controls and fire/life-safety systems.
  • Define commissioning and acceptance tests that demonstrate the contractual operating mode, not only a short unloaded or partial-load run.
  • Check the operating model: staffing, procedures, remote monitoring, data ownership, alarm response and escalation.
  • Stress the financial model against fuel interruption, lower net output, longer maintenance, delayed spares and replacement-power exposure.
  • Translate gaps into conditions precedent, hold points, reserves, contingencies or additional inspection.

Common mistakes and red flags

  • Using an OEM datasheet as if it were a site-specific performance guarantee.
  • Treating nameplate capacity as dependable net export.
  • Assuming every lost operating hour is an engine failure.
  • Accepting a maintenance budget without linking it to the exact configuration and duty.
  • Leaving gas quality, cooling and grid responsibilities outside the main technical review.
  • Using a single availability percentage without defining denominator, exclusions and responsible causes.

Practical decision checklist

  • Can every material model input be traced to a contract, measured record, serial-specific document or labelled assumption?
  • Are gross, net, dependable and contracted capacity kept separate?
  • Are fuel, ambient, grid and maintenance constraints modelled explicitly?
  • Do commissioning tests prove the intended operating mode?
  • Are unresolved risks reflected in financing conditions, reserves or contingencies?

When remote review is sufficient

A remote review can test the document set, model assumptions, scope interfaces, fuel and performance basis, maintenance plan and evidence gaps. It is often enough to reject an incoherent project basis before expensive site work.

When physical inspection is required

Site work is required where the decision depends on installed condition, construction progress, equipment identity, commissioning performance, fuel sampling, electrical tests or operator capability that cannot be established from records.

Conclusion

Decision-grade due diligence connects the financial model to the physical system that must produce the cash flow. The critical output is not a long risk list; it is a set of evidence-backed conditions that the investment decision can use.

FAQ

Is technical due diligence the same as a feasibility study?

No. A feasibility study builds and tests a project basis; technical due diligence independently challenges an existing basis for an investor, lender, buyer or owner. The scopes can overlap, but the decision, evidence set and reporting role should be stated in writing.

Can due diligence be completed remotely?

A substantial desk review can be completed remotely when the data room contains traceable fuel, equipment, design, model and maintenance evidence. Site verification is needed for conclusions that depend on installed condition, construction progress, sampling, testing or operator capability.

What should happen to an unsupported assumption?

Classify it as an unresolved input, quantify the decision sensitivity where possible and assign a verification action, owner and deadline. It should not be presented as a measured or vendor-confirmed fact.
