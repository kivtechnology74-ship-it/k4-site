Expectation vs. Reality: Three Jenbacher J320 Gensets After 303 Days of Operation

The engines ran more hours than planned but averaged 84.7% of rated output while running. What that changed in energy, EBITDA and modelled return.

Igor Shibanov · CEO K4-TECHPublished: 29 October 2026Evidence checked: 29 October 2026
K4 FIELD DATAK4 MODELASSUMPTIONS SHOWN

Key findings

  • The engines ran more hours than the feasibility study assumed: 96.3% of calendar time against 94.5% in our recalculation of the study.[9][20]
  • They delivered less output while running: 900 kW per engine on average, 84.7% of the 1,063 kW rating, against 95% assumed. In July and August output fell to about 74% of rating.[11][12]
  • Annual energy came in about 9% below the recalculated study. Lower output while running explains almost all of the gap. Stops explain very little of it.[15]
  • The modelled observed-operation case produces year-1 EBITDA 8.5% below the recalculated study on the investor-reported price basis: USD 1.37M against USD 1.49M. The nominal local-currency pre-tax IRR falls from 40.1% to 33.6%. This is a model result, not actual site EBITDA.[28][29]
  • The site gas meter cannot prove any fuel saving, and 44% of lost hours have no recorded cause. Part of the study still cannot be verified because the plant's records do not allow it.[17][24]

A feasibility study (FS) for a power plant with three Jenbacher J320 gas gensets promised a 2.38-year payback, 95% of rated output while running and 0.271 Nm³ of gas per kWh.[1][3] We tested those assumptions against the plant's daily operating log for all three units over 303 days, 18 laboratory oil analyses, and a K4-TECH model that recalculates the study on the investor-agreed basis.[7]

The study was neither simply right nor simply wrong. The engines rarely stopped, but they delivered less energy per running hour than assumed. An investor needs to separate these two effects, along with gas supply, ambient heat, consumer demand, maintenance timing and the quality of the records.

All money values are in US dollars, converted from the local-currency model at the official central-bank rate of 28 October 2026 (80.5307 local-currency units per USD). USD equivalents are presentation conversions at the stated rate, not a separate USD-denominated investment model.[30]

1. The promise: what the feasibility study assumed

Physics. Three J320 units of 1,063 kW electrical and 1,000 kW thermal each, 3,189 kW installed. Gas: 274 Nm³/h per unit at full load. Availability 0.95, operating load 0.95 (95% of rated output while running), 58% of recovered heat used. Running hours were 8,125 per unit per year (92.8% of the calendar), producing 24.6 GWh a year at 0.271 Nm³/kWh.[2][3]

Prices (excl. VAT). Gas USD 0.088/Nm³, electricity valued at USD 102/MWh as avoided grid purchases, heat USD 6.2/Gcal.[4]

Money. CAPEX USD 3.5M (USD 1,110 per installed kW; the VAT basis of CAPEX is not stated). EBITDA USD 1.49M a year, payback 2.38 years. The study did not calculate an IRR.[1][5]

Why we recalculated it. The study's payback is CAPEX divided by an EBITDA that combines revenue averaged over ten years with costs from a different period. It costs gas at full-load consumption for every running hour and schedules maintenance by project year rather than by running hours.[6] The K4-TECH model keeps the study's physics but applies the investor-agreed basis: commissioning on 1 October 2025, a five-year horizon, 10% annual indexation, a 10% discount rate, 20% profit tax, 10-year depreciation, prices net of 18% VAT, and the major overhaul at 80,000 running hours.[7] On that basis the study's expectation is year-1 EBITDA of USD 1.49M and a nominal pre-tax IRR of 40.1%.[28] The VAT and profit-tax rates are the investor's planning assumptions; section 8 also shows a scenario at the higher statutory rates now in force in the project's jurisdiction.[31]

2. The evidence: 303 days, three engines, a log and 18 oil analyses

The operating log covers 2 November 2025 to 31 August 2026: 303 calendar dates for each of the three units, or 21,816 possible unit-hours.[8] For each unit and day it records running hours, cumulative hours, a gas-meter reading, generated kWh and a free-text note. The plant entered service on 1 October 2025, so its first month is not in the log.[7]

The records do not contain: the gas metering basis and heating value, the point where kWh are metered, actual heat use, actual revenue and full operating costs, a cause for almost half of the lost hours, and any maintenance entry after 6,666 running hours.

3. Hours: utilisation and output while running are different numbers

The three units ran 21,008.5 of 21,816 possible unit-hours: 96.3% utilisation, or about 8,436 hours per unit per year.[9] That is better than the 94.5% in the recalculated study and the 92.8% in the original.[20]

Output while running is a separate number. The units averaged 900 kW while running: 18.9 GWh over 20,985 running hours with a meter reading, or 84.7% of rating.[11] A model or contract that uses one "availability" figure for both would be wrong in both directions. (Engineers often reserve "load factor" for output over the whole period, including stops; we use "average output while running" to avoid that ambiguity.)

Correction. An earlier K4-TECH case page on this plant reported 96.5% and 759 lost unit-hours. Those figures combined a numerator and a denominator built on different date bases. On a consistent basis the plant lost 807.5 unit-hours and utilisation was 96.3%. The case page has been updated.[10]

4. Output: nameplate MW versus delivered MWh

Over 303 days the plant generated 18.9 GWh, about 22.8 GWh a year. The recalculated study expects 25.1 GWh gross, so actual output is about 9% lower.[14][15]

The monthly picture explains why. Output per running engine peaked at 980 kW (92% of rating) in March. It fell from May, reached 82% in June and stayed near 74% in July and August. Running hours hardly changed: 2,176 unit-hours in March against 1,989 in July.[12] The plant was not standing still. It was running at part load.

The operator attributes the summer decline to two causes: high outdoor temperature and a lower power draw by the consumer's load.[13] The log does not let us split the two. For a miner or a data-centre developer the lesson is direct: you are paid for delivered kWh, not nameplate kW, and summer is when both the engines and the IT load are under most stress.

5. Gas: the manufacturer curve is the defensible modelling basis — not measured site consumption

There are three gas figures, and they must not be mixed.

  • The study: 0.271 Nm³/kWh, calculated as full-load gas for every running hour.[3][6]
  • The manufacturer's performance curve: at about 900 kW, consumption is about 236 Nm³/h, or 0.262 Nm³/kWh, at a reference heating value of 9.5 kWh/Nm³. This is the fuel input used in our model. It is a benchmark, not a measurement.[16]
  • The site meter: the operating log implies approximately 0.240 m³/kWh, but the metering basis and reference conditions have not been verified.[17]

At the reference heating value, 0.240 m³/kWh would mean about 44% electrical efficiency. That is above the engine's rating at full load and not credible at part load.[18] The meter volume must therefore be on a different basis, or the meter and the kWh measurement cover different boundaries. We do not treat the meter figure as evidence that the plant used less gas than expected. Its trend is still useful: gas per kWh rose by about a fifth from winter to July as output fell, which is what part-load operation does.[19]

External constraints account for 300 to 383 unit-hours, or 1.4–1.8% of possible hours. At the investor-reported prices that is worth about USD 23,000–29,000 a year in the model.[21][22] The engines themselves (maintenance and repairs) cost 70 recorded hours. The largest block, 354.5 hours, has no cause in the log, so it may include engine events we cannot see.[24]

Some stops started on the consumer's side: a short circuit, burnt contacts in the consumer's container, and a reduction of the consumer's load. For a plant serving a mining or data-centre load, the consumer's wiring and protection are part of plant reliability. The contract should say who carries these stops, and who carries gas-supply interruptions.

7. Maintenance: running hours versus calendar planning

Each unit accumulated about 7,000 running hours in 303 days. All three passed 3,333 hours in March 2026 and 6,666 hours on 31 July or 12 August 2026. The log shows short maintenance stops near 3,333 hours on two units but no maintenance entry after 6,666 hours on any unit.[25]

At about 8,400 hours a year the next thresholds come quickly: 10,000 hours around December 2026, 20,000 hours around March 2028, and 40,000 hours around July 2030 — inside a five-year horizon.[26] The investor plans the major overhaul at 80,000 hours, outside the horizon. The scope of the 40,000-hour service still moves the result: with the planned scope the pre-tax IRR is 33.6%, with no work it would be 34.4%, and with a full 40,000-hour package 30.2%.[27] A plan built on calendar years would not show this.

The oil analyses support the engines' condition. Across 18 samples, wear metals stayed low (iron ≤5 ppm, copper ≤4 ppm, silicon ≤8 ppm), with no water or glycol. The latest samples, at 2,449–2,576 hours of oil service, were still rated normal, with base number down 27–34% from fresh oil.[23] One sample ID showed magnesium of 61–88 ppm without an explanation. The lab uses five sample IDs for three engines, so we do not attribute individual samples to individual engines.

In this case, electricity value and output while running move the model more than the recorded engine-related downtime. Equipment reliability was not tested as a separate variable, and 354.5 lost hours have no recorded cause.[27]

10. What could and could not be verified

Verified: running hours, kWh, stop notes, oil-lab values, and the model mechanics (we recomputed the IRR independently from the monthly cash flows).

Not verified: the gas metering basis and heating value; whether kWh are metered at the generator terminals or after auxiliaries; actual heat use, revenue and operating costs; the cause of 354.5 lost unit-hours; maintenance after 6,666 hours; the first month of operation; and our own earlier hour count, which we have corrected.

None of these gaps is dramatic on its own. Together they show why one register — keyed to engine and running hours, linking every stop with its cause, every maintenance event and every oil sample — matters more than any single figure in a feasibility study.

11. Questions an investor, miner or data-centre developer should ask

  1. Is there a daily log from a comparable plant? Ask for hours, kWh, gas and a cause for every stop. Without it, 95% output while running is an assumption. We saw 85%.
  2. What is the output in summer at my site? Ask for the derating curve against ambient temperature and the cooling design. We saw about 74% of rating in July and August.
  3. In which cubic metres is gas metered, at what heating value, and where is the meter? Without this, gas consumption and efficiency cannot be checked.
  4. Is maintenance placed by running hours? At 8,400 hours a year, ask when the 40,000-hour service falls inside your horizon, what it includes and who pays for it.
  5. Which kWh price is used, with or without VAT? Is it a tariff, an avoided cost or a mining equivalent?
  6. Who carries gas-supply, grid and consumer-side stops? In our case gas supply was the largest known cause of lost hours.
  7. Pre-tax or post-tax, and at which rates? Ask for the discount rate, the tax rates actually in force, depreciation, residual value and the treatment of VAT on CAPEX.
  8. Is the IRR in local currency or in USD? A local-currency IRR with 10% indexation cannot be compared with a USD hurdle rate.

Conclusion

Over 303 days, three J320 engines ran more than the feasibility study assumed but delivered less output while running, mainly because of summer heat and a smaller consumer load. Recorded external stops were real but small in money terms. In the observed-operation model, year-1 EBITDA is about 8.5% below the recalculated study on the investor-reported price basis, and the pre-tax IRR falls from 40.1% to 33.6%. These are modelled results: actual revenue and costs were not available, and the gas meter and almost half of the lost hours remain unverified.

Next steps: confirm the gas metering basis and heating value; obtain heat-use, revenue and cost data; explain the unrecorded stops; record maintenance by running hours; add the first month and the months after August to complete a full year; and rerun the model with the full monthly profile.

FAQ

How did three Jenbacher J320 gensets perform against their feasibility study? Over 303 days they ran 96.3% of possible hours, above the 94.5% in the recalculated study, but delivered 900 kW on average while running (84.7% of the 1,063 kW rating) instead of the assumed 95%. Annual energy was about 9% below expectation. In the observed-operation model, year-1 EBITDA is about 8.5% lower on the investor-reported price basis; this is a modelled, not an actual, figure.

What is the difference between utilisation and average output while running? Utilisation is the share of calendar hours an engine runs. Average output while running is the share of rated output it delivers when it runs. This plant had high utilisation (96.3%) but lower output while running (84.7%). A model that uses a single "availability" figure for both will be wrong in both directions.

Why did the plant produce less in summer? The operator reports two causes: high outdoor temperature and a lower power draw by the consumer's load. Output per running engine fell to about 74% of rating in July and August. The log does not allow the two causes to be separated.

Did the engines use less gas than the feasibility study assumed? The study assumed 0.271 Nm³/kWh. The manufacturer's performance curve gives about 0.262 Nm³/kWh at 900 kW, and that is the fuel input in our model. The site meter implies about 0.240 m³/kWh, but its metering basis is not verified; at the reference heating value it would mean an electrical efficiency of about 44%, above the engine's rating. We do not treat the meter figure as evidence of a fuel saving.

What caused the lost hours? 807.5 unit-hours were lost: gas supply 166 h, ambient heat 60 h recorded plus 83 h inferred, thunderstorm 47 h, grid and consumer side 27 h, planned maintenance 41 h, repairs 29 h, and 354.5 h with no recorded cause.

When does the 40,000-hour service fall for a J320 running about 8,400 hours a year? About 4.8 years after commissioning — inside a typical five-year investment horizon. In this case the major overhaul is planned at 80,000 hours, but the scope of the 40,000-hour service still moves the pre-tax IRR between 30.2% and 34.4%.

Is the IRR in this article a USD IRR? No. It is a nominal local-currency IRR with 10% annual indexation. USD figures are presentation conversions at the official central-bank rate of 28 October 2026 and are not a separate USD investment model.
