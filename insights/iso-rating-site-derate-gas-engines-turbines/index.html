Catalogue MW Is Not Site MW: ISO Ratings, Derating and Dependable Output

“ISO conditions” are not one condition — and a published rating is not a project guarantee.

Igor Shibanov · Founder and Technical AdvisorPublished: 23 October 2026Evidence checked: 23 October 2026
PRIMARY SOURCESSITE RATINGK4 ANALYSIS

Key takeaways

  • A published MW figure applies at a stated reference condition, on stated fuel and at a stated measurement boundary. It is not automatically the net output available at site.
  • A public Caterpillar engine sheet declares 25 °C and 100 kPa under ISO 3046/1, while the gas-turbine ISO reference point is 15 °C, 101.325 kPa and 60% relative humidity.[3][4][5]
  • Rating bases can be configuration-specific. INNIO’s published Type 4 hot-and-humid sheet, for example, states electrical output at no more than 50 m altitude and 35 °C combustion-air temperature.[6]
  • Altitude, ambient conditions, fuel, cooling, emissions settings, inlet and exhaust losses and auxiliary consumption can all change the output or efficiency that belongs in the financial model.
  • Before shortlisting equipment, require every option to be restated at one site design point and at the same net delivery boundary.

The nameplate is a sentence with the conditions removed

Every power project passes through a moment where a plant becomes a number: so many megawatts, at such an efficiency, for such a price. The number is then treated as a permanent property of the machine.

It is not. It is declared output at specified conditions, on specified fuel and at a specified boundary. Change the altitude, ambient temperature, humidity, gas composition, cooling arrangement or boundary and the published figure may no longer describe the installation. The magnitude and direction for a particular machine come from the supplier’s project-specific data and correction method, not from a universal percentage.

This is not a theoretical distinction. If reference output is carried directly into a site model, the project can overstate contracted capacity, understate auxiliary demand, misstate fuel cost or write a performance guarantee against conditions the site does not experience.

What the standards and public data actually establish

Three standards groups frame the comparison, and they do not share one reference point.

Reciprocating engines. ISO 3046-1:2002 provides additional requirements for declarations of power and consumption, working with the general engine-power framework in ISO 15550:2016.[1][2] A public Caterpillar gas-engine technical data sheet makes the practical basis explicit: the rating is presented under ISO 3046/1 at standard reference conditions of 25 °C and 100 kPa barometric pressure, with an instruction to consult the altitude-deration chart beyond the rated altitude or temperature.[3]

Gas turbines. ISO 3977-2:2023 is the current procurement standard for gas-turbine reference conditions and ratings; it replaced the withdrawn 1997 edition.[4] ISO’s publicly accessible reference-condition definition states 15 °C, 101.325 kPa and 60% relative humidity for ambient or compressor-inlet air.[5] That is a different reference day from the public engine sheet above.

Generating-set classifications. ISO 8528-1:2018 defines classifications for the application, rating and performance of reciprocating-engine-driven AC generating sets. It remains current while a revision is under development.[7] The applicable duty and rating classification should therefore be stated in the offer rather than inferred from a headline output.

A developer comparing an engine plant and a turbine at face value may therefore be comparing figures declared against different ambient conditions and possibly different equipment boundaries. Both offers must first be normalised to the same project basis.

The rating basis is configuration-specific

Even within one engine platform, the output basis can vary by configuration. INNIO’s published Type 4 hot-and-humid sheet states electrical output at no more than 50 m above sea level and no more than 35 °C combustion-air temperature, while also noting that the technical data and fuel consumption are stated according to ISO 3046.[6]

This is good engineering disclosure, not an OEM defect. It shows why the buyer must read the document covering the exact configuration offered. A family brochure, an old project data sheet and a project-specific performance schedule may all describe the same engine designation while carrying different output, emissions, cooling or fuel assumptions.

The same principle applies to gas. The public Caterpillar sheet ties tolerances to fuel quality, specifies a methane-number tolerance and states its emissions basis at the engine exhaust flange before after-treatment.[3] Those values belong to that data set; they are not universal limits for gas engines. What transfers to other projects is the method: demand the fuel basis, pressure range, variability limits and emissions reference conditions behind the quoted performance.

What this means for the developer, owner or investor

Are all options being compared on the same day? Require output, heat rate and emissions at one written site design point: altitude, ambient design temperature and percentile, humidity, gas analysis, cooling basis and inlet/exhaust arrangement. Until then, the comparison table contains answers to different questions.

Is the figure gross or net, and at which boundary? Site export is affected by fans, pumps, gas compression, treatment, transformers, controls and other auxiliary loads. Inlet and exhaust losses also depend on the installation. The contractually relevant value should be defined at the project’s metering or delivery boundary.

Does the rating classification match the duty? Baseload, variable-load, peak, standby and grid-parallel duties do not create the same technical or contractual commitment. The intended annual hours, load profile, starts and operating mode must be reconciled with the classification used in the supplier’s offer.

Which site parameters move the number

Altitude and ambient temperature. Lower air density reduces available mass flow. Gas turbines are particularly sensitive to compressor-inlet conditions; reciprocating engines are constrained by the turbocharging, charge-air and cooling system selected for the project. Neither should be corrected using a generic percentage copied from another model.

Humidity. Humidity changes air properties and can affect performance and emissions. Its effect is model- and control-specific and should remain in the supplier’s calculation rather than be treated as a universal rule.

Cooling. A packaged engine rating does not design the project’s radiators, dry coolers, pumps or heat rejection. Charge-air and jacket-water temperatures must remain within the limits assumed by the project-specific performance data at the selected ambient design point.

Fuel. Gas composition, methane number, heating value, contaminants, delivery pressure and variability can change permissible settings, output, efficiency, treatment scope and emissions. Send the complete gas analysis and expected variation range; a fuel label such as “natural gas” is not a performance specification.

Emissions. A quoted concentration must be compared with the permit on the same oxygen, dry/wet and reference-condition basis. A tighter limit may require changed engine settings, after-treatment or a different operating point, each with project consequences.

Five ways the wrong rating basis distorts the model

  1. Fleet sizing. A plant sized from reference MW can miss required net capacity at the design ambient. The risk becomes a larger fleet, reduced firm output or a contractual shortfall.
  2. Fuel budget. Heat rate carried directly from reference data may misstate lifetime fuel cost. Use the project-specific curve at the outputs and conditions the plant will actually hold.
  3. Performance guarantees. If the supplier guarantee and offtake obligation use different conditions or boundaries, the owner retains the gap. State guaranteed net output and heat rate at site conditions, with the correction method annexed.
  4. Used-equipment suitability. A machine selected for a coastal project is not automatically suitable at altitude on different gas. Cooling, turbocharger matching, gas train, generator, control settings and emissions scope belong to the original project and must be reconciled with the destination.
  5. Emissions compliance. Comparing unlike oxygen or reference bases can create false comfort or a false problem. Normalise the values before selecting settings or after-treatment.

For used equipment, this rating-basis review belongs alongside identity, lifecycle and condition checks. K4’s technical due-diligence scope and the published configuration-mismatch case show why condition alone does not establish destination fit.

A verification sequence that takes days, not months

  1. Fix one site design point in writing and circulate it to every bidder.
  2. Require every quotation to be restated at that point with the correction method attached.
  3. Separate machine rating from net export and itemise the auxiliary load.
  4. Confirm the rating classification against the intended duty.
  5. Send the full gas analysis and variability range, not only the fuel name.
  6. For used equipment, reconcile the original site, fuel, cooling and emissions basis with the destination.
  7. Carry the site figures into the financial model and write the guarantee on the same basis.

The same inputs also belong in the project’s wider platform-selection process, lifecycle and reliability model and, for compute projects, the AI power architecture review.

Frequently asked questions

Are gas engines and gas turbines rated at the same reference conditions?

They should not be assumed to be. A public Caterpillar engine sheet declares 25 °C and 100 kPa under ISO 3046/1, while the gas-turbine ISO reference point is 15 °C, 101.325 kPa and 60% relative humidity. Project comparisons should verify each document and restate every option at one site design point.

Can catalogue output be used as guaranteed site output?

Not without confirming the site conditions, fuel, cooling arrangement, inlet and exhaust losses, auxiliary load and measurement boundary. The supplier should provide project-specific output and heat-rate data with the applicable correction method.

What information is needed for a site-rating check?

At minimum: altitude, ambient design temperature and percentile, humidity, complete gas analysis and pressure range, cooling basis, emissions limit, inlet and exhaust arrangement, auxiliary loads, duty profile and the contractual delivery boundary.

Conclusion

Nothing here requires a universal derate percentage. It requires preserving the conditions attached to every number as that number travels from a data sheet into a comparison, a financial model and a performance guarantee.

The strongest project basis is simple: one site design point, one delivery boundary, one fuel range and project-specific supplier data for every competing option. Catalogue MW is an input. Dependable net MW at site is the decision.
