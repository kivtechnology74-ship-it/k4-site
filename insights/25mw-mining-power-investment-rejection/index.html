25 MW Mining Power: When the Investment Screen Says Stop

A proposed 25 MW grid-parallel plant was not built. The interview links the decision to a lower grid offer and financing cost; modelled debt cases are explicitly illustrative, not actual lender terms.

K4 TechnologyInterview-based analysis · 2026

Discuss an investment screen

A 2019 mining-power concept reviewed retrospectively by K4 in 2026. The proposed project was not built; this page does not describe a present-day K4 contract or delivery.

The investment question

Could approximately 25 MW of new captive generation compete with the site's grid supply after the tariff changed—and could the project support debt service on verified cash available for debt service?

What was reported

Reported: A mining and processing plant with an estimated load of about 100 MW considered six MWM TCG 2032 units, approximately 25 MW in total, operating in parallel with the grid. The interview described an estimated CAPEX of around RUB 1 billion including VAT. The project was not built.

The participant recalled that the grid offer changed from RUB 4.00 to RUB 3.60/kWh, including VAT, after the utility learned of the generation plans. Own-generation cost was estimated at approximately RUB 3.00/kWh, but its VAT basis and cost boundary are unknown. These are interview-based figures, not reviewed bills, bids or contracts.

How the financing screen works

Calculated illustration: At 25 MW and 8,000 operating hours, a RUB 0.60/kWh gross spread gives RUB 120 million per year before taxes, residual grid charges, auxiliary loads, working capital, maintenance, fees and other reconciliations. This is arithmetic under stated assumptions, not verified cash available for debt service.

Illustrative financing: A hypothetical 70% loan at 12% over ten years produces an annual annuity payment of roughly RUB 124 million. The gross-spread-to-payment ratio is about 0.97×. This is not formal DSCR: neither the spread nor CFADS is verified, and no actual term sheet was reviewed. The scenario does not reconstruct historical IRR or NPV.

Because the grid tariff is explicitly VAT-inclusive while the generation-cost basis is not known, the apparent spread may not compare like with like. The model therefore illustrates sensitivity to financing; it does not prove why the project failed a formal underwriting test.

The value of preserving the alternative

The interviewee linked the utility's lower price to the customer's generation plans. If the customer continued buying grid electricity at the improved tariff, that concession may have had value without the proposed CAPEX. The tariff agreement, purchase volume and discount duration are unavailable, so the value cannot be quantified or independently attributed to the feasibility work.

The investment lesson is broader than “reject projects with expensive debt”: validate the grid alternative, align VAT and cost boundaries, stress debt service against verified CFADS and preserve a credible no-build option.

What an investment committee should request

  • 15-minute load, self-consumption, import/export and power-quality data.
  • Utility tariff offer, contract duration, discount conditions and remaining grid charges.
  • OEM fuel curve, fuel-quality basis, service/overhaul scope and auxiliary consumption.
  • CAPEX breakdown, schedule, contingencies, interconnection and balance-of-plant scope.
  • Consistent VAT, tax and working-capital treatment.
  • Actual lender terms, fees, covenants, reserves and a CFADS-based debt model.

Evidence boundary

Prepared from a retrospective interview. No utility bills, tariff contract, OEM offer, design drawings, operating data, bank proposal or financial statements were reviewed. Historical observations are interview reports; cash-flow and debt cases are calculations or assumptions. The project was not built. This is not an engineering audit, credit opinion, investment recommendation or K4 project execution claim.
