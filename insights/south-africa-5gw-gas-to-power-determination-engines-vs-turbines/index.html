South Africa’s 5 GW Gas-to-Power Determination

Where reciprocating gas engines could compete with gas turbines — and where they do not.

Igor Shibanov · Founder and Technical AdvisorPublished: 22 October 2026Evidence checked: 22 October 2026
PRIMARY SOURCESTECHNOLOGY SELECTIONK4 ANALYSIS

Key takeaways

  • The 5,000 MW is determined capacity, not a single tender, award or construction programme.
  • The gas allocation in this determination sits in 2031–2037. Earlier capacity may come through other routes, including procurement under way, public-sector capacity, private generation or other mechanisms.
  • IRP 2025 calls for 6 GW of CCGT by 2030, with a 50% minimum load factor for the initial programme and scope for more flexible operation later.
  • Technology selection should begin with duty, fuel, site, redundancy and lifecycle requirements — not with an OEM shortlist.

On 7 October 2026, South Africa’s Minister of Electricity and Energy announced the proposed approach to the first Section 34 determination under IRP 2025. On 14 October, the Energy Storage and Gas New Generation Capacity Determination was formally gazetted as Notice 8021 in Government Gazette No. 55543. It determines 9,600 MW of new generation capacity: 4,600 MW of energy storage and 5,000 MW of gas. Procurement is to take place through one or more IPP procurement programmes, with NTCSA or a successor designated by the Minister as buyer and the Department of Electricity and Energy or its designated successor as procurer. The electricity must be purchased from independent power producers.[1]

A determination is an authorisation, not a tender. No single 5 GW gas tender was created by the notice, and no procurement under this new determination had been announced on the publication date. The capacity is determined; it is not procured, not awarded and not under construction. Those are different stages with different risk profiles.

The year columns matter as much as the headline totals. The storage allocation runs from 2026 to 2042; the gas allocation sits in 2031–2037.[1] Read plainly, the authorised gas capacity is a 2030s build. Capacity required earlier could come through other routes, including procurement already under way, public-sector capacity, private generation or other mechanisms.

The department’s own statement says procurement design will address start-up times, ramping capability and minimum operating levels, while affordability will be evaluated against the expected operating profile.[3] Those are machine and plant-architecture characteristics. Technology selection is therefore an input to the business case, not a downstream purchasing detail.

Dispatchable capacity and a constrained grid

The policy logic is explicit: curtailment is rising, and usable electricity is being left unused while system and contractual costs remain. Storage can shift surplus energy within its duration. Gas is intended to supply the remaining requirement when renewable output declines, tying its value to flexible, dispatchable operation rather than to a technology label.[3]

The network remains the binding constraint. NTCSA’s current execution plan targets 14,494 km of transmission line and 132,650 MVA of transformation capacity by 2034.[4] Separately, the South African government has estimated that transmission expansion will require approximately R440 billion over ten years.[5] These figures describe different things and should not be presented as one NTCSA-sourced data point.

For developers, the practical implication is that connection capacity will remain scarce and unevenly distributed. Generation that can be placed near load, built in stages and operated flexibly carries a different delivery risk from a large project dependent on one connection arriving on schedule.

What the new determination actually means

Five separate tracks are routinely merged in commentary. They should remain distinct.

  1. The determination. It establishes that capacity is needed, names the buyer and procurer, and authorises one or more IPP procurement programmes. It does not itself create a bid window.[1]
  2. Procurement already under way. GASIPPPP Bid Window 1 is a separate, pre-existing process for 2,000 MW. Bids closed on 29 May 2026. On 22 October, the official programme site published the bids received but no preferred-bidder announcement.[6]
  3. The IRP 2025 trajectory. The gazetted plan identifies 6 GW of gas CCGT by 2030 and a 50% minimum load factor for the initial gas-to-power programme. It also requires built-in flexibility to adjust that load factor and contemplates variable-load-factor facilities later.[2]
  4. Political and infrastructure statements. LNG import nodes, pipelines and regional trade may influence the market, but an ambition is not a procurement commitment.
  5. The private market. Behind-the-meter and wheeled private generation follows different offtake, financing and timing logic from a national IPP programme.

The technical parameters of GASIPPPP BW1 must be checked against the current amended RFP before they are used in a project model. The public site confirms the programme and milestones; access to the full RFP is limited to registered bidders.[6]

Gas engines versus gas turbines is not an OEM question

A common project-development sequence is to shortlist an OEM, obtain a budget price, build the financial model around it and examine the operating architecture afterwards. The architecture then has to survive what the model has already promised.

Availability, maintenance philosophy, part-load behaviour and redundancy are not properties of a supplier alone. They are properties of a configuration: how many units, of what class, operated how often, maintained under which strategy, with how much spare capacity. Two credible offers at the same price per kilowatt can carry materially different operating risk.

A financially attractive project can still be built on the wrong generation architecture.

The operating characteristics that matter

The comparison is useful only parameter by parameter and only when machine class is stated. A 2 MW spark-ignited engine, an 18 MW medium-speed engine, a 30 MW aeroderivative turbine and a 300 MW heavy-duty frame unit are not interchangeable categories.

Granularity. Reciprocating plants reach capacity by multiplying smaller units. A turbine project usually builds around fewer, larger blocks. That difference affects redundancy, phased delivery, connection strategy and the share of the plant lost when one unit trips.

Part-load behaviour. A multi-engine plant can shed load by stopping units and keep the remaining machines closer to an efficient operating point. A simple-cycle turbine may lose efficiency more sharply at reduced load, while emissions-compliant minimum load is machine-specific. Published OEM curves for the selected model and site conditions should govern the model.

Site and fuel conditions. Both technologies derate with altitude and temperature, and both have fuel limits. Turbine output is strongly influenced by inlet-air density and mass flow. Reciprocating engines are also subject to turbocharging, cooling and knock constraints linked to methane number and gas-composition stability. Turbines have their own Wobbe-index, pressure and contaminant limits. Neither technology can be assessed from a universal rule of thumb.

K4's ISO rating and site-derate framework sets out the evidence needed to compare those conditions on a consistent basis.

Maintenance architecture. Engine plants normally involve more frequent, shorter, unit-level interventions while the remaining units continue to operate. Turbines may have fewer but deeper outages that remove a larger block. Neither pattern is automatically better; they create different availability and spares strategies.

Fuel flexibility. Pipeline gas, regasified LNG and any future indigenous gas will not necessarily have the same composition, pressure or variability. The fuel specification belongs in the technology decision before commercial terms are fixed.

Where reciprocating engines may have an advantage

These are conditions to test, not conclusions:

  • Modular developments where several standard units fit the required net output.
  • Projects with a firm N+1 or N+2 requirement and a low acceptable loss per unit.
  • Variable-load or residual-load duty where plant-level part-load efficiency matters.
  • Fast-start, cycling and hybrid solar/BESS/gas operation.
  • Data centres, mines and industrial sites requiring concurrent maintainability or staged expansion.
  • Projects where unit-by-unit commissioning can advance time to first power.
  • Behind-the-meter generation designed around availability to a specific load rather than fleet-average output.

Every item remains configuration-dependent. Fuel supply, grid connection, permitting and balance-of-plant delivery can erase an apparent equipment lead-time advantage.

Where gas turbines may still be the better choice

Large single blocks. At several hundred megawatts behind one connection, a small number of large machines may be simpler to build, connect, operate and staff than dozens of units.

Combined cycle and sustained duty. IRP 2025 frames the initial 6 GW specifically as CCGT, with flexible dispatchable operation but a 50% minimum load factor for the initial programme.[2] Where annual running hours and steam-cycle economics justify it, combined-cycle efficiency is difficult for a simple-cycle reciprocating plant to match on fuel cost alone. The same policy also allows the load factor to be adjusted and considers variable-load-factor facilities later, so the duty should not be reduced to a single baseload assumption.

Steam demand, footprint and balance of plant. Sites needing large quantities of high-pressure steam, fewer exhaust trains and auxiliaries, or a compact large-block layout may favour turbines. Engine heat recovery can support steam or hot-water systems, but a larger share of engine heat is available at lower temperatures.

Why modularity matters in a constrained grid

Modularity can match installed capacity to the connection actually granted, allow revenue at partial capacity and separate the technical risk of one unit from the commercial risk of the whole plant. It can also support a staged investment decision while grid allocation, fuel contracts and offtake mature on different schedules.

That optionality is not free. More units can mean higher capital per kilowatt, more auxiliaries, more maintenance events and greater operating complexity. Where phasing, redundancy and partial-load performance are not valuable, paying for them is the wrong decision in the opposite direction.

Gas plus BESS plus renewables

At system level, storage and gas have different jobs. Storage shifts energy within its duration and responds quickly; gas provides dispatchable energy over longer periods, limited by fuel availability rather than state of charge. The determination allocates them separately and does not imply co-location.

Where solar, BESS and thermal generation do serve one load, the thermal plant follows the residual profile: low during the solar day, high through the evening ramp, with starts, stops and part-load hours. The feasibility study should calculate equivalent starts and maintenance consequences for the specific machine, and it should model efficiency at the outputs the plant will actually hold — not only at rating.

The relevance for data centres, mining and industrial power

Private projects are not buying the same product as a national IPP. They need capacity near a particular load, a defensible availability basis, concurrent maintainability, staged expansion and a time-to-power aligned with a commercial commitment.

For a data centre, modularity may make N+1 or N+2 practical, permit maintenance without a full-plant outage and follow IT-load growth. But the answer also depends on the uptime requirement, grid strength, UPS/BESS architecture, firm gas supply, emissions permit and electrical topology. K4’s data-centre captive-power framework sets out those checks. Mining and industrial projects should likewise test fuel credibility, cooling, deployment boundaries and operating responsibility; see the onsite gas-power due-diligence framework.

New versus low-hour equipment

Under schedule pressure, suitable low-hour equipment may reduce lead time and acquisition CAPEX. Neither benefit is automatic. A mismatched unit can cost more in reconfiguration, refurbishment and delay than a new order.

Checks include serial-number identity, operating hours, overhaul position, preservation, voltage, frequency, cooling, generator rating, gas setup, controls supportability, emissions compliance, auxiliaries, documentation, transport and warranty. A factory acceptance test shows that a machine performs under test conditions; it does not establish provenance, lifecycle position, site suitability or scope completeness.

K4’s published evidence includes a desk review that stopped an unsuitable purchase before inspection, a six-engine pre-commissioning audit and a three-engine operating-data case. These examples support a method; they do not turn one OEM or one plant history into a universal technology claim.

Technical due diligence before procurement

A defensible sequence is:

Load profile → gas composition and supply conditions → site conditions → reliability target → operating philosophy → plant configuration → engine or turbine class → OEM → specific equipment → CAPEX/OPEX model → due diligence → procurement.

The project should define net MW at the delivery point, hourly and seasonal load, residual load after renewables and storage, redundancy, availability measurement, grid/island operation, voltage and frequency, gas composition and pressure, altitude and temperature, emissions, annual hours, starts, maintenance strategy, lifecycle horizon, financing constraint and required time to power. If one of those inputs is blank, competing offers may be priced but are not yet comparable.

Conclusion

South Africa has authorised new dispatchable capacity while the grid remains constrained and renewable curtailment is rising. Some projects will be large, centralised and best served by turbines, particularly where CCGT and sustained load apply. Others — especially captive, hybrid and staged projects where redundancy or variable load dominates — may be better served by modular reciprocating plants.

The decision is project-specific and technical. It should be made before capital is committed, not justified afterwards.
