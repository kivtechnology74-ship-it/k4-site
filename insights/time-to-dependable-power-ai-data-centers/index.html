Time to Dependable Power: The Metric AI Data Centers Should Actually Compare

First power is not dependable power. Compare the IT megawatts delivered by date, under a defined workload and availability obligation.

Igor Shibanov · Founder and Technical AdvisorPublished: 27 October 2026Evidence checked: 27 October 2026
PRIMARY SOURCESK4 MODELASSUMPTIONS SHOWN

Summary. First power is not dependable power. For AI data centers, the investable metric is dependable IT megawatts delivered by a defined date, under a defined workload and availability obligation. Whether reliability or speed should dominate depends on who pays for delay: a GPU owner with hardware on site and a powered-shell developer reach different answers from the same plant.

First MW is not dependable MW

Most onsite-power debates for AI campuses start with the wrong question: which technology gives power fastest, or which is cheapest per kilowatt. Both questions count the generator. The customer counts something else—the IT load that stays powered when units trip, gas pressure drops or a GPU cluster swings tens of megawatts in a second.

Williams’ Socrates project for a Meta campus in New Albany, Ohio, makes the gap visible. Williams describes two 200 MW sites,[1] while its 2026 investor materials report 556 MW of combined ISO generation capacity, excluding BESS.[2] The resulting 1.39 ratio illustrates the gap between publicly stated project capacity and installed ISO capacity, although the public sources do not fully reconcile the measurement boundaries. ISO rating, net output at site conditions, committed capacity and the delivery point are four different numbers—exactly the problem.

The metric I propose is dependable IT megawatts delivered by a defined date, under a defined workload and availability obligation. Every word removes a way to make an option look better than it is.

  • Dependable excludes reserve, derate and auxiliary losses.
  • IT megawatts exclude cooling and PUE.
  • By a date puts time-to-power inside the metric instead of beside it.
  • Under an obligation forces the owner to say what “reliable” means before choosing equipment.

For a transparent calculation path, see the companion technical note: model, inputs and comparison tables. The earlier AI Compute Power Reality Check explains why power availability should be tested before compute CAPEX.

Three layers of reliability

“Reliable power” hides three different quantities, and vendors, plant operators and tenants each quote a different one.

  1. Unit reliability—how often one engine or turbine trips and how long it takes to return. This is what an OEM data sheet and a service contract describe.
  2. Plant availability—how many hours and events leave the running fleet unable to carry the required load, and whether the plant stays stable when one unit drops out. This depends on unit count, unit size relative to load, controls and switchgear.
  3. Fuel-to-IT resilience—how many minutes a year the IT load actually loses power, how many events cause that loss, and how long the longest lasts. This includes fuel, generation, medium-voltage distribution, UPS and batteries, any grid tie and the control software linking them.

AI load stresses the second and third layers more than conventional IT. Microsoft, OpenAI and NVIDIA describe synchronized training jobs that swing “tens or hundreds of megawatts” at roughly 0.2–3 Hz, a range that can excite torsional resonance in turbine-generator shafts.[5] Meta reported instant fluctuations “on the order of tens of megawatts” while training Llama 3.[6]

The third layer is also where common-mode failures live. In Winter Storm Elliott, natural-gas fuel issues represented 20% of all causes of US generation outages, derates and failed starts, and about 83% of the fuel-related causes.[7] No amount of N+k protects twelve engines that share one vulnerable gas header.

Same availability, different risk: a transparent N+k example

Take an islanded plant that needs ten units to carry full load, with each unit unavailable 2% of the time because of forced outages. The table shows what additional spare units buy. These are K4 model results under stated assumptions, not measurements from an operating site.

Configuration Hours per year below full capacity
N+1 (11 units) 171
N+2 (12 units) 13.5
N+3 (13 units) 0.9

Assumptions: each unit has 2% forced unavailability; failures are independent; planned maintenance and common-mode events are excluded. Event frequency and duration, which require repair-time assumptions, are modelled in the technical note.

Availability alone does not describe risk. The same deficit hours can arrive as many short events or a few long ones. Two fleets calibrated to the same availability can have very different outage profiles.

A deficit hour is not automatically an IT outage. If one equal-sized unit is missing, training load can be curtailed by about 10% or a battery can carry the gap until reserve starts. Curtailment of AI load at 25% for three hours has been demonstrated in a utility program.[11]

Every unit trip is still a transient. Even with reserve, the remaining units and battery must ride through every trip—dozens a year in a plant of this size. How many reach the IT load depends on unit size, battery sizing and controls. No operator publishes that figure yet; it is the figure I would ask any bidder to defend.

A month of delay vs a lifetime of outages: it depends who pays

The claim that AI data centers need reliability above everything and the counter-claim that speed beats everything are both wrong as general rules. The value of one month differs by party.

The delay values below are K4 estimates. For the GPU owner they represent depreciation and cost of capital on $30–45M of IT equipment per megawatt. For the compute operator they represent lost contribution using a $2.2–4.5 per H100-hour screening range,[12] 60–90% utilization and 60–80% margin; this overlaps with the owner case and is never added to it. For the developer, the model uses assumed rent of $140–200 per kW-month before lease delay damages.

Moving the ten-unit island from N+1 to N+2 costs about $26–63k per dependable IT MW per year in capital charge. For training load that can be curtailed, it avoids about $15–105k a year of curtailment and restart cost. The net result is roughly −$48k to +$79k a year, or −$0.33M to +$0.54M over 15 years at a 12% discount rate. The extra unit may or may not pay for itself. It is a borderline call, not a rule.

  • GPU owner: one month of delay outweighs even the best case for the extra unit. Speed wins once the design clears a defined reliability floor.
  • Powered-shell developer: the best case for the extra unit equals roughly three months of rent, and the tenant will price outage risk into the lease. Reliability weighs at least as much as speed.
  • Inference and SLA-bound load: each event carries customer and contract consequences a curtailment model cannot capture. N+2 should be tested as the minimum islanded screening case; a grid tie or battery can change the result.

Power caused 45% of operators’ most impactful data-center outages in 2025, and one in five impactful outages cost more than $1M.[8] The question is never “reliability or speed” in the abstract. It is how much reliability this obligation needs, and how soon this party needs it.

Three public strategies

Three of the best-documented US AI campuses with onsite gas show different answers to the trade-off. None publishes achieved availability, so these are readings of design choices, not performance verdicts.

Socrates, Ohio (Williams for Meta): reliability designed in. The plant is islanded—Ohio siting material says it would not be physically connected to the electric grid. The design uses multiple generating technologies, a mobile spare, diesel black-start capability and two dedicated gas pipelines.[4] This may reduce some equipment-specific failure modes while increasing controls, spares and skills complexity. Public sources do not establish whether the two pipelines eliminate upstream common-mode exposure. Williams reported phase one complete within budget and on time, with phase two on track for the fourth quarter of 2026.[3]

Stargate, Abilene, Texas (Crusoe for OpenAI): grid plus onsite. The campus connects to ERCOT and adds aeroderivative and industrial gas turbines onsite. Crusoe ordered 29 LM2500XPRESS units of about 35 MW for its AI data centers—GE does not say all are destined for Abilene—which GE says can start independently of the grid in five minutes.[9] Grid access plus onsite generation can reduce single-source exposure, although the paths are not necessarily independent of every common-mode event and large units make each trip a large share of an island load.

Colossus, Memphis and Southaven (xAI): speed first. Turbines went in before permanent permits, full grid capacity and permanent units, according to permit records and satellite analysis; Tesla Megapacks were added at the first site.[10] The strategy won the market window. It also created additional permitting and litigation exposure.

The pattern: speed is a legitimate first phase, but only if the permanent architecture is already decided and the bridge fits inside it.

Five questions to answer before choosing an OEM

  1. What exactly is the obligation? State annual unserved IT minutes, maximum events and longest permitted event by hall and workload. “99.99%” alone is ambiguous—it still allows about 53 minutes a year, in one block or fifty.
  2. What happens when the largest unit trips at peak load? Ask for a plant-level dynamic study, not a unit data sheet. Start times, ramp rates and permissible load steps are different numbers; the GPU load profile decides which binds.
  3. Which single event takes out more than the reserve? Walk the chain from gas supply to rack: one pipeline, one header, one control system, one MV bus, one cold snap.
  4. When is the obligation met, not when is first power? A bridge should use the permanent single-line, be permitted for its unit count and period, and satisfy: months gained × delay value > bridge cost − residual value + extra outage exposure.
  5. What does a delivered dependable megawatt-hour cost? Include capital per dependable IT MW, fuel at real heat rate, O&M per operating hour, spares and lease-engine pools, and expected unserved energy at its true value. For the islanded gas-engine case we estimate 1.4–2.1 MW installed per dependable IT MW; other technologies need their own conversion.

If a proposal cannot answer these five, its price and lead time are not yet comparable with anything. K4’s captive gas-power framework provides the project-level checks behind the shortlist.

Independent review on the payer’s side

Across his career, K4 founder Igor Shibanov has participated hands-on in approximately 40 MW of gas-engine generation projects across Jenbacher, MTU, Caterpillar, MWM, Rolls-Royce and Chinese platforms, from project management through operation and major overhauls. What trips island plants is rarely the headline technology choice. It is a unit sized for availability instead of the load, a gas contract written for phase one, or a spares plan nobody priced.
