Used J320 Corrosion Case: Why Handover and Preservation Evidence Matter

A post-purchase case showing what happens when condition evidence is not preserved across handover, transport and storage

WhatsApp ↗ceo@k4-technology.com
K4 TechnologyPublished: 13.09.2026PROCESS LESSON
OEM DATAFIELD EVIDENCEK4 ANALYSIS

A used Jenbacher J320 purchased in Latvia later developed significant corrosion around the spark-plug-well area.

Transaction correspondence stated that:

  • the unit arrived without spark plugs;
  • the machine was then stored in a humid warehouse;
  • preservation was not properly documented;
  • after approximately eight months, substantial rust / corrosion was observed.

At first glance, the obvious conclusion might be:

“The warehouse caused the corrosion.”

But K4 later reviewed photographs that also raised a different possibility:

at least part of the deterioration may have existed before storage.

The retained evidence does not establish a single definitive cause.

And that is exactly why this case matters.

If condition is not documented at handover and arrival, later damage can become impossible to attribute.

Short Answer

Can you prove whether corrosion on a stored used J320 was pre-existing or caused by storage?

Not reliably if the condition was not documented at handover and arrival.

Without:

  • dated photographs;
  • open-port inspection;
  • spark-plug / well condition records;
  • borescope evidence where justified;
  • preservation documentation;
  • arrival-condition report;

the evidence chain may be too weak to separate:

  • pre-existing deterioration;
  • transport exposure;
  • storage damage;
  • mixed causation.

What Happened

The retained correspondence states that a used J320 had been purchased in Latvia earlier in 2025.

Later, on 12 December 2025, the transaction discussion described the following sequence:

  1. the machine arrived without spark plugs;
  2. the buyer did not carry out a complete inspection / preservation process on arrival;
  3. the unit was stored in a humid warehouse;
  4. after roughly eight months, significant corrosion / rust was observed around the spark-plug-well area.

The same discussion explicitly noted that the unit should have been checked before storage and preserved.

At that stage, however, the key problem was no longer simply the presence of corrosion.

The bigger problem was:

When did it begin?

Why the Evidence Chain Broke

A used gas engine changes hands across several risk stages:

seller condition → loading → transport → unloading → arrival → storage → recommissioning

If the condition is not documented at each critical handover point, any later defect can become an attribution problem.

For this J320, the retained materials do not establish a complete dated evidence chain showing:

  • exact condition immediately before dispatch;
  • exact condition at loading;
  • exact condition on arrival;
  • spark-plug / open-well condition at handover;
  • preservation actions immediately after receipt;
  • humidity / storage environment over time;
  • internal condition at the start of storage.

Without those reference points, later corrosion is real — but causation becomes uncertain.

What K4 Later Observed

K4 later reviewed photographs that were not available to the end customer at the original decision point.

Those photographs raised concern that at least part of the deterioration may have pre-dated the storage period.

This does not prove pre-existing corrosion.

It means the later evidence was consistent with more than one explanation.

Possible scenarios included:

Scenario A — mainly pre-existing condition

Some corrosion / deterioration was already present before handover.

Scenario B — mainly storage-related

The lack of plugs / protection and humid storage materially created or accelerated the corrosion.

Scenario C — mixed causation

Pre-existing deterioration was present and was then accelerated during transport / storage.

The retained evidence does not allow K4 to select one of these scenarios as definitively proven.

Evidence Classification

VERIFIED

The retained case materials support that:

  • a used J320 had been purchased in Latvia earlier in 2025;
  • the later correspondence described the unit as arriving without spark plugs;
  • humid storage was reported;
  • preservation / inspection on arrival was described as inadequate;
  • significant corrosion / rust was later observed;
  • photographs of the deterioration are retained;
  • K4 later reviewed additional visual evidence and questioned whether at least some deterioration may have pre-dated storage.

REPORTED / NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED

The correspondence reported:

  • exact storage conditions;
  • the absence of spark plugs on arrival;
  • the timing of storage and discovery;
  • the buyer-side preservation process.

These points form part of the transaction record, but this public case does not independently reconstruct every step from third-party custody logs or dated inspection reports.

NOT VERIFIED

The retained evidence does not establish:

  • when the first corrosion began;
  • what percentage of the damage was pre-existing;
  • what percentage resulted from storage;
  • whether transport contributed;
  • a single responsible party;
  • a definitive causal chain.

K4 ANALYSIS

The strongest conclusion is procedural:

The evidence chain was not strong enough to attribute the later condition confidently.

That is the failure.

The lesson is not:

“Always blame storage.”

The lesson is:

document condition before the asset changes custody or environment.

Why Spark-Plug Wells and Openings Matter

When a used engine is transported or stored, open or poorly protected ports can create exposure paths for:

  • humidity;
  • condensation;
  • contamination;
  • debris;
  • corrosion.

The exact preservation method depends on the asset, configuration and OEM requirements.

K4 does not apply one universal preservation procedure to every Jenbacher.

But the due-diligence principle is universal:

openings, ports and storage condition must be documented before responsibility becomes ambiguous.

Handover Is Part of Due Diligence

Many buyers think due diligence ends when the purchase decision is made.

It does not.

The technical risk chain continues through:

  1. seller handover;
  2. loading;
  3. transport;
  4. unloading;
  5. arrival inspection;
  6. preservation;
  7. storage;
  8. recommissioning.

For a used gas engine, these stages can create new defects — or make it impossible to distinguish new defects from old ones.

A strong transaction therefore needs a condition record at more than one point.

Recommended Evidence Chain

At minimum, document:

Before dispatch

  • general machine condition;
  • engine openings / ports;
  • spark-plug wells;
  • visible corrosion;
  • oil / coolant state;
  • preservation condition;
  • container / package condition.

At handover / loading

  • dated photographs;
  • damage / exception record;
  • agreed packing / sealing status.

At arrival

  • condition before cleaning or repair;
  • open ports / covers / plugs;
  • signs of water / humidity exposure;
  • transport damage;
  • visible corrosion;
  • oil / coolant condition.

Before storage

  • preservation actions;
  • storage environment;
  • humidity / ventilation where relevant;
  • closure / sealing of openings;
  • periodic preservation requirements if applicable.

Before recommissioning

  • inspection;
  • borescope where justified;
  • fluids;
  • corrosion check;
  • rotation / mechanical checks as applicable;
  • updated condition record.

K4 Decision Framing

NOT A PRE-PURCHASE VERDICT — PROCESS LESSON

This case is not used to say:

  • BUY;
  • DO NOT BUY;
  • seller caused the damage;
  • storage caused all damage.

It is used to show:

what happens when inspection, handover and preservation evidence are not continuous.

The technical problem was corrosion.

The transaction problem was uncertainty about causation.

What Was Not Established

The retained evidence does not establish:

  • a single cause of corrosion;
  • the exact start date of deterioration;
  • seller fault;
  • buyer fault;
  • transport fault;
  • storage as the sole cause;
  • pre-existing corrosion as the sole cause.

Any public version must preserve that uncertainty.

Buyer Takeaways

1. Inspect again at handover

A pre-purchase inspection does not replace a dated arrival-condition record.

2. Photograph before cleaning, repairing or storing

Once the condition changes, the original baseline can be lost.

3. Protect openings immediately

Open wells / ports should never be treated as a minor logistics detail.

4. Storage is a technical condition, not just a warehouse decision

Humidity, preservation and idle periods can materially change asset condition.

5. Evidence protects both sides

A strong handover record helps determine whether a defect was:

  • pre-existing;
  • transport-related;
  • storage-related;
  • or mixed.

6. If causation cannot be proven, do not pretend it can

The correct engineering conclusion can be:

cause unresolved.

That is stronger than an unsupported accusation.
