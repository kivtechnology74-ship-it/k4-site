An earlier version reported 96.5% engine-hour utilisation and 759 lost unit-hours. Those figures divided recorded hours from all 303 reporting dates by possible hours from 302 dates. What actually stops a high-utilisation gas-engine power plant? The answer is not available from a count of engine failures alone.

This anonymized case uses K4 field operating data from a three-engine distributed power plant. Its daily log records fuel-supply interruptions, heat limitations, weather and grid events, scheduled work, repairs and records that cannot be assigned to a specific cause.

Dataset and methodology

K4 reviewed the daily operating log for each unit. The log spans 303 calendar reporting dates, giving 21,816 possible unit-hours across three units. Recorded running time totals 21,008.5 unit-hours; the difference is 807.5 lost unit-hours.

The resulting 96.3% is engine-hour utilisation calculated from the operating log. It is not contractual availability, technical availability, guaranteed availability, OEM reliability or a general statement of fleet reliability.

Downtime labels are K4’s analysis of the daily log and include external causes. K4 inferred 83 heat-related hours from the repeated daytime pattern where the log contained no note; those hours remain explicitly identified as inference.

Headline operating results

Across the reporting record, the three units accumulated 21,008.5 engine-hours and generated 18,892 MWh. The generation meter has one unit-day without a reading; dividing the matched production by matched running hours gives 900 kW per running engine, or 84.7% of rating.

The operating log shows high utilisation. It does not show that every lost hour was an engine event.

Interpretation

The largest category remains deliberately neutral: 354.5 hours have no recorded cause. They should not be presented as equipment failure. The separate 83-hour ambient-heat category is K4 inference from the repeated daytime pattern and is not upgraded to a logged fact.

Among identified causes, gas supply was largest at 166 hours. Recorded ambient heat, thunderstorms and grid or consumer events together accounted for another 134 hours. Planned maintenance represented 41 hours. Repairs and other causes represented 29 hours.

Plant output depends on an operating system beyond the engines. Fuel infrastructure, cooling and ambient design, weather resilience, grid interface, consumer demand, maintenance planning and data quality all affect realised utilisation.

Normalized fuel benchmark

This is an engineering-normalized reference benchmark, not measured site gas consumption.

The site’s recorded gas-meter totals are excluded because they are inconsistent with the electrical production data. No actual site electrical efficiency is calculated from those readings.

The manufacturer/project performance curve gives 274 Nm³/h at 1,063 kW, 211 Nm³/h at 796 kW and 148 Nm³/h at 529 kW, using a reference gas LHV of 9.5 kWh/Nm³. Interpolation at approximately 900 kW gives approximately 236 Nm³/h.

Engineering lessons

  1. Define the metric. Running hours divided by possible unit-hours is utilisation, not a contractual or OEM reliability measure.
  2. Separate engine and system causes. Fuel, ambient, weather and grid constraints can stop production without being engine failures.
  3. Keep unknowns visible. Partial-day and incomplete records should remain neutral until evidence supports a cause.
  4. Treat maintenance separately. Scheduled intervention and corrective repair answer different operating questions.
  5. Normalize cautiously. A performance-curve fuel benchmark supports comparison, but cannot replace validated metering.

The operating question

A headline utilisation figure is useful only when its basis is explicit and the lost hours are investigated. This record does not support reducing downtime to “engine failures”. It supports protecting fuel supply, designing for ambient conditions, planning for weather and grid events, scheduling maintenance, investigating repairs and improving log completeness.
