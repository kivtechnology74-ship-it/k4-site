Six Engines Were Only Part of the Risk

A buyer was evaluating an unfinished six-unit gas-engine power plant. The installation looked advanced, but visible progress did not establish whether the engines were ready, the balance of plant was complete or the transaction evidence was sufficient.

A visually advanced installation can contain three different liabilities: stored-engine condition, unfinished balance of plant and transaction-document risk.

The investment question

The buyer needed to know whether a partially completed plant justified further investment. Confirming that six engines were physically present was not enough. The decision required four evidence streams: engine condition, plant systems, work remaining before commissioning and transaction-critical documentation.

What was installed

The plant comprised six used J612 units on individual foundations. Inspected infrastructure included mechanical ventilation, separate high- and low-temperature cooling circuits, control and synchronisation cabinets, energy metering and 6.3 kV switchgear.

Why “almost complete” was not commissioning-ready

At the inspection date, open work remained on the oil filling, draining and make-up system; thermal protection around indoor exhaust sections; pneumatic actuation of three-way valves; and the external fuel-gas connection. The indoor exhaust routing also required further engineering review.

This case does not state a percentage complete, cost to complete or later commissioning status. Those outcomes were not established in the publishable evidence.

Borescope inspection of all six engines

K4 examined the cylinder groups of all six engines. No critical cylinder-group wear was identified during that inspection. Individual cylinders did show scoring associated with loosened carbon deposits, and rust was visible on some liners. Deposits on valves, cylinder heads and piston crowns were consistent with the operating-history range observed in the inspection record and had begun to loosen during storage and mechanical handling.

Evidence boundary

Observed condition did not prove full engine health.

A borescope finding is one evidence layer. It does not establish the condition of every component, future remaining life or the root cause of liner rust. Condensation during storage and coolant ingress through aged seals remained possible explanations, not proven causes.

The remediation scope

K4 recommended removing the cylinder heads for deeper assessment, cleaning and evaluating the cylinder heads, pistons and liners, and replacing liners or piston assemblies where condition required.

  • Pressure-test all plate heat exchangers because leakage traces were visible across the inspected group.
  • Replace missing starter batteries.
  • Replace batteries serving safety systems that were beyond their service date.
  • Close the remaining oil, exhaust, pneumatic and external gas-system work before commissioning.

The buyer did not progress the transaction after the technical exposure and a separate site-document issue were considered together. The legal particulars are intentionally not described because they are outside the publishable evidence set.

Lessons for buyers and developers

  • Inspect all engines in a multi-unit offer; sampling can hide unit-to-unit differences.
  • Separate engine condition from balance-of-plant completion.
  • Treat storage history and preservation as technical evidence, not as seller narrative.
  • A commissioning plan needs a closed remediation scope, not only engines that can be turned over.
  • Technical findings and transaction-document risks must meet in the same decision.
